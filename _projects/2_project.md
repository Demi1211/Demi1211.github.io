**2023–2026 · with Jian Meng, Yuling Zhong, Xiaodong Xu, and Dr. Edith Kaan**

#### Question

Judging that an action is wrong requires knowledge of the world and of how people typically behave—the same kind of knowledge we use to decide whether a sentence makes sense. Are moral violations then just a special case of semantic anomaly, or does the brain treat them differently?

#### Design

Native English speakers read short texts for comprehension while we recorded EEG. Depending on the preceding context, the same critical word made the text a **moral violation**, a **semantic violation**, or a neutral control, which allowed a direct comparison between the two kinds of violation. We analyzed the P200, N400, and Late Positive Component (LPC) time windows with trial-based models, and also collected moral-acceptability and plausibility ratings for every item.

#### Findings

- **Different brain responses:** moral violations elicited a long-lasting posterior LPC starting around 300 ms, whereas semantic violations elicited a positivity that started later and was descriptively more frontal.
- **Moral judgments explain the LPC:** LPC amplitudes were explained by moral-acceptance scores over and above plausibility scores, but not the other way around.

These results suggest that processing moral deviations engages at least some mechanisms that differ, in nature and timing, from those used for semantic oddities.

#### My role

I designed experimental sentences and study materials (flyers, eligibility questionnaires, protocols), collected EEG data, and related the N400 and P600/LPC responses to word surprisal from large language models.
