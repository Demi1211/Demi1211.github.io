**2024–2026 · with Emiliana Pulido, María Josefina Estrada, Souad Kheder, and Dr. Edith Kaan**

#### Question

A prevalent idea in bilingualism research is that switching between languages draws on the same domain-general control system we use to resolve conflict in non-linguistic tasks. If so, juggling two languages should "warm up" that system and make the next non-verbal conflict easier to resolve. Earlier evidence for this cross-task adaptation is mixed, and almost all of it comes from *comprehension*. Within the Adaptive Control Hypothesis, *dense* code-switching—where both languages are woven tightly together within a sentence—is thought to rely on cooperative rather than competitive control, so it is not obvious that it should engage inhibition at all. We asked whether *producing* dense code-switches changes performance on a following Flanker trial.

#### Design

Thirty-six Spanish–English bilinguals (from 43 recruited, after preregistered exclusions) read sentences aloud and then responded to a Flanker trial (e.g., `→ → ← → →`). Each sentence was either unilingual Spanish or densely code-switched (e.g., *El gato estuvo tirado on the floor tomando sol and resting después de la cena de la tarde*). Participants also completed a bilingual verbal-fluency task, the Bilingual Language Profile, and the Bilingual Code-Switching Profile, so we could look at individual differences in dominance and code-switching habits.

#### Findings

- **Production was harder in dense code-switching:** participants articulated dense code-switched sentences more slowly than unilingual ones.
- **A robust Flanker effect, but no adaptation:** incongruent trials were slower than congruent ones, but the linguistic context of the preceding sentence did not modulate this conflict effect.
- **No change over the session:** participants simply got faster in the second half; there was no sign that adaptation emerged as exposure to code-switching accumulated.
- **Individual differences:** neither language dominance nor code-switching experience reliably interacted with the effects.

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/projects/cs-flanker-rt.jpg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" alt="Violin plots of Flanker reaction times by condition and language context" caption="Flanker reaction times by condition (congruent vs. incongruent) after dense code-switched vs. unilingual sentences." zoomable=true %}

Together, the results give no evidence that producing dense code-switches recruits inhibitory control that carries over to a non-linguistic task. They fit the view that dense code-switching relies on language-internal control processes, and they highlight methodological limits on detecting cross-task adaptation in production.

#### My role

I led the project from conception to publication: designed the paradigm and stimuli, programmed the experiment in E-Prime, developed the coding scheme for switching errors, self-corrections, and hesitations, ran the mixed-effects analyses in R, managed IRB approval, and trained and mentored two undergraduate research assistants (both co-authors).
